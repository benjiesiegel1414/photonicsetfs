Co-Packaged Optics Explained: The Technology Behind Photonics ETFs

Co-packaged optics moves the laser link from a plug-in module to the chip itself. Here is how it works, why AI data centers want it, who makes the parts and which photonics ETFs hold them.

By Benjie Siegel · November 20, 2026 · 14 min read · Live data

If you read anything about photonics ETFs, you run into three letters over and over: CPO. Co-packaged optics is the technology that turned a quiet corner of the optical parts industry into one of the most watched themes of 2026, and it is a big part of why 9 photonics ETFs holding $685M exist at all.

This guide explains co-packaged optics in plain English. You do not need an engineering background. By the end you should understand what problem CPO solves, how it is different from the plug-in modules used today, which companies make the pieces, what the forecasts say, and how all of that connects to the funds on this site. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell anything.

The short version: AI data centers move so much data that the links between machines now use a real share of the power budget. Co-packaged optics shortens the electrical path by putting the light-making parts right beside the switch chip. That saves power and improves signal quality, but it is harder to build and service, so it is rolling out slowly next to the plug-in modules that still dominate.

The problem: AI moves more data than copper can carry

Inside a computer chip, data travels as electrical signals over tiny copper wires. That works well over a few millimeters. It works poorly over longer distances at high speed, because copper loses signal strength and turns part of the energy into heat. The faster you push data, the shorter the distance copper can handle.

Light does not have that problem. A pulse of laser light can travel through a glass fiber for hundreds of meters, or hundreds of miles, with very little loss. So data centers have used fiber optics for years to connect racks of servers to each other.

AI changed the scale. Training a large AI model means wiring together hundreds of thousands of graphics processors so they act like one giant machine. Every one of those processors has to exchange data with the others constantly. The network that connects them is no longer a side detail. It is a major part of what the system costs to build and to power.

How major? Nvidia's Ian Buck told IEEE Spectrum that the plug-in optical modules in an AI data center use roughly 10% of the total power going to the processors. For a facility with 400,000 processors, that works out to about 40 megawatts spent just on optical links, and more than half of it goes to the lasers inside the modules.[2] Forty megawatts is enough to run a small town. That is power the operator pays for and cannot use for computing.

How optical links work today: pluggable transceivers

To see what CPO changes, start with what it replaces. Today's standard part is the pluggable optical transceiver. It is a metal module about the size of a pack of gum that slides into a socket on the front of a network switch. A fiber cable plugs into the other end.

Inside the module are a laser, a modulator that stamps data onto the light, a detector that reads incoming light, and a digital signal processor chip that cleans up the signal. The switch chip, which sits in the middle of the circuit board, sends its data as electrical signals across the board to the module at the front panel. Then the module converts those signals to light.

The weak spot is that trip across the circuit board. It may only be 15 to 30 centimeters, but at today's speeds that copper path loses a lot of signal. The signal processor chip in the module exists largely to repair the damage, and it burns power doing it.

Pluggables have real strengths, which is why they have lasted. If one fails, a technician pulls it out and slides in a new one in seconds. Operators can buy them from many competing vendors. And they can mix speeds and reaches in the same switch. These are not small advantages, and they are the main reason CPO has been "a few years away" for most of a decade.

What co-packaged optics changes

Co-packaged optics removes the long copper trip. Instead of sending electrical signals across the board to a module, the optical engine is mounted on the same package as the switch chip, a few millimeters away. Light, not electricity, carries the data off the chip package and out to the front of the box.

Most CPO designs rely on silicon photonics, which means building the optical parts on silicon wafers using the same kind of factories that make ordinary chips. That allows many optical channels to be packed into a tiny area next to the switch chip.

There is one thing silicon cannot do well: produce light. So the laser is usually a separate part made from a material called indium phosphide. In many CPO systems the lasers sit in their own replaceable module at the front of the switch and feed steady light to the optical engines through fiber. That keeps the part most likely to fail, the laser, easy to service, while the rest is fixed in place.

This detail matters a lot for investors. It means CPO does not remove the need for laser makers. It changes what kind of lasers they sell: fewer, more powerful, more expensive ones.

The numbers Nvidia put on it

The moment CPO went from research topic to product roadmap was March 2025, when Nvidia announced two families of switches with co-packaged optics, called Quantum-X Photonics and Spectrum-X Photonics. Nvidia said that compared with traditional pluggable designs, its CPO switches use four times fewer lasers and deliver 3.5 times better power efficiency, 63 times better signal integrity, 10 times better network resiliency at scale and 1.3 times faster deployment.[1]

Those are a vendor's numbers about its own products, so treat them as claims. But the direction is consistent with what others report. Micas Networks, which builds a CPO switch around a Broadcom chip, has claimed a 40% power saving compared with pluggable systems.[2]

Nvidia also named the partners building these systems with it: TSMC, Browave, Coherent, Corning, Fabrinet, Foxconn, Lumentum, SENKO, SPIL, Sumitomo Electric and TFC Communication.[1] That list reads like a photonics ETF holdings page, and we will come back to it.

Nvidia said the InfiniBand version would arrive later in 2025 and the Ethernet version in 2026.[1] So as of this writing, CPO is shipping in early volumes. It is not theoretical anymore, and it is not everywhere either.

Why CPO is not taking over overnight

If CPO saves that much power, why is not every switch built this way already? Four practical reasons.

Serviceability

When a pluggable module fails, you replace the module. When a co-packaged optical engine fails, it is attached to one of the most expensive chips in the building. Vendors have worked around this with detachable optical engines and external laser modules, but data center operators are cautious people. They want years of reliability data before they bet a whole facility on a new design.

Heat

Lasers do not like heat, and a high-end switch chip is one of the hottest parts in a data center. Putting optics millimeters from that chip is a hard thermal problem. It is one reason Nvidia's first CPO switches are liquid-cooled.[1]

Supply chain habits

With pluggables, an operator buys switches from one company and modules from several others, and plays them against each other on price. CPO bundles the optics with the switch. Buyers give up some of that flexibility.

Pluggables keep improving

The old approach is not standing still. Broadcom has said a newer signal processor could cut power by more than 20% in the fastest transceivers.[2] A design called linear pluggable optics removes that chip entirely to save power. Every improvement to pluggables pushes out the point where CPO is the only option.

Not every expert is convinced the current approach scales forever, either. One researcher quoted by IEEE Spectrum doubted that silicon modulators can go much beyond 400 gigabits per second per lane, which would mean new materials are needed for later generations.[2] That uncertainty is a risk for some companies and an opening for others.

What the forecasts say

Forecasts in a fast-moving field are educated guesses, and they have been wrong about CPO before. With that warning, here is what two of the most followed research firms were saying.

Yole Group expects the market for photonics packaging to grow from about $4.5 billion a year to $14.4 billion by 2031. Inside that total it sees transceivers at about $8 billion and co-packaged optics at about $5 billion, up from virtually zero in early 2026.[3]

Two things stand out. First, CPO goes from nothing to a real market in about five years. Second, plain transceivers are still the bigger segment at the end of the period. The realistic picture is both technologies growing together, not one killing the other.

LightCounting, another research firm that tracks the optical parts market, projected sales of Ethernet optical transceivers growing 30% to 35% a year in 2025 and 2026, then slowing to 15% to 20% a year from 2027 to 2030. It called that a possible soft landing, and it noted that this market has historically hit a decline about every three years, most recently in 2019 and 2022. LightCounting also said it expects CPO to become the best option for the short links inside AI clusters.[4]

A Zacks summary of the theme in September 2026 cited an estimate that CPO could reach roughly 20% to 30% penetration by 2028, with adoption speeding up in 2027.[7] That is a faster ramp than the Yole numbers imply, which tells you how wide the range of opinions is.

Who makes what: the co-packaged optics supply chain

CPO is not one product from one company. It is a stack of parts, and different photonics ETFs lean on different layers of it.

Lumentum is a good example of how CPO shows up in a real company's numbers. In its results for the quarter ended March 28, 2026, Lumentum reported revenue of $808.4 million, up about 90% from a year earlier. It said it expected a purchase order worth several hundred million dollars for ultra-high-power lasers for CPO in the first half of 2027, disclosed a $2 billion direct investment from Nvidia, and bought a factory in North Carolina to convert into its fifth indium phosphide fab.[5] The company described itself as shipping well below demand.

That is one company in one quarter, and a strong quarter does not make a stock a good investment at any price. But it shows that the laser makers are central to CPO, not casualties of it.

What co-packaged optics means for photonics ETFs

None of the funds on this site report a number for "CPO exposure." You have to look at what they hold. A few patterns are worth knowing.

The concentrated AI optics funds. $LYTE puts most of its weight in a short list of laser and module companies, led by Lumentum, Coherent and Ciena. If CPO lasers and optical engines become as large as the bulls expect, those are among the most direct public ways to own it. The flip side is concentration: a few names drive the whole fund.

The supply chain funds. $LAZR reaches further up the chain into the wafer and equipment makers such as Aixtron, AXT and LandMark Optoelectronics. Those companies sell to every laser maker, so they are a bet on total laser volume more than on any one design winning.

The fund with a private CPO developer. $LUMA listed Ayar Labs, a private company working on optical chip-to-chip links, as its largest position at about 12% of assets in the issuer's November 2026 holdings.[6] That is direct exposure to next-generation optical links that you cannot buy on a stock exchange, with the valuation questions that come with any private holding.

The index fund. $PHOX tracks an index that mixes the laser and module makers with networking chip and connector companies such as Broadcom, Credo and Amphenol. Broadcom is one of the main switch chip designers building CPO products, so the index catches that side of the story too.

The lithography fund. $EUV is mostly chip equipment, but it holds TSMC as its largest position, and TSMC is on Nvidia's list of CPO manufacturing partners. Corning, Lumentum and Coherent are smaller positions.

Winners and losers are not obvious

It is tempting to draw a simple map: CPO good for A, bad for B. The reality is messier, and it is worth thinking through before you decide a fund is "the CPO play."

  • Module makers face the most change. Companies whose main business is assembling pluggable modules could see some of that work move inside the switch. But the big Chinese module makers, including Zhongji Innolight and Eoptolink, are named in the CPO supply chain as well, and pluggable volumes are still rising.
  • Laser makers may sell fewer units at higher prices. CPO uses fewer, more powerful lasers. Whether that is better or worse for revenue depends on pricing that is still being set.
  • Switch chip designers gain control. When the optics ship inside the switch, the company selling the switch captures more of the value. That favors Nvidia and Broadcom, which are large companies that most photonics ETFs hold lightly or not at all.
  • Timing can hurt even if the thesis is right. A technology can win over ten years while the stocks tied to it fall 40% in a bad quarter. Optical stocks did fall hard in October 2026. Our guide to photonics ETF risks covers that in detail.

How to use this as an investor

You do not need to predict which optical design wins to make sense of photonics ETFs. A few practical questions get you most of the way.

  1. Which layer do you want? Lasers and modules, materials and equipment, chips, or a mix. Match the fund to the layer by reading its top holdings, not its name.
  2. How concentrated are you willing to be? Some funds put more than half their weight in three or four stocks. Others spread it out.
  3. Do you want a manager or a rulebook? Active funds can shift as the technology shifts. An index fund follows its rules whatever happens. Our guide to how photonics ETFs differ walks through that choice.

The bottom line

Co-packaged optics is a real technology solving a real problem: AI networks use too much power pushing data over copper. Moving the optics next to the chip fixes much of that, and the largest chip company in the world has put products and a partner list behind it.

It is also early, hard to manufacture and competing against a pluggable design that keeps getting better. The forecasts call for fast growth from a tiny base, with plenty of room to be wrong on timing.

For anyone looking at photonics ETFs, the useful takeaway is this. CPO does not make one fund the obvious choice. It explains why these companies are getting so much attention, and it gives you a better set of questions to ask when you open a fund's holdings list.

Frequently asked questions

What is co-packaged optics in simple terms?

Co-packaged optics, or CPO, puts the part that turns electrical signals into light right next to the switch chip instead of in a removable module at the front of the box. A shorter electrical path wastes less power and loses less signal.

Is co-packaged optics replacing pluggable transceivers?

Not quickly. Pluggable modules are still the standard and are selling in record volumes. Most forecasts see CPO growing from close to zero today into a multi-billion dollar market around 2030 while pluggables keep growing next to it.

Which companies benefit from co-packaged optics?

Laser makers such as Lumentum and Coherent, fiber and connector makers such as Corning, assemblers such as Fabrinet, foundries such as TSMC and Tower Semiconductor, and switch chip designers such as Nvidia and Broadcom are all part of the CPO supply chain.

Which photonics ETF has the most co-packaged optics exposure?

No fund reports CPO exposure as a number. The funds built around AI data center optics, including LYTE, PHOX, LAZR and LUMA, hold the laser, module and materials companies most tied to it. LUMA also holds private CPO developer Ayar Labs.

When will co-packaged optics ship in volume?

Nvidia said its first co-packaged InfiniBand switches would arrive in 2025 and its Ethernet versions in 2026. Yole Group expects CPO packaging revenue to reach about $5 billion by 2031 from virtually zero in early 2026.
