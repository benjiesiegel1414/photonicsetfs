Photonics ETFs vs. Semiconductor ETFs: What Is Actually Different?

If you already own SMH or SOXX, do you need a photonics ETF? A side-by-side look at what each one holds, what it costs, how concentrated it is and where the two overlap.

A lot of people who land on this site already own a semiconductor ETF. Funds like $SMH and $SOXX have been some of the best ways to ride the AI buildout, and they are in millions of portfolios. So the natural question when a new theme shows up is: do I already own this?

For photonics, the answer is mostly no, with a couple of important exceptions. This guide compares photonics ETFs and semiconductor ETFs on what they hold, what they cost, how concentrated they are and how they have behaved, so you can see where they overlap and where they do not. It is a comparison for research purposes, not a recommendation to buy, sell or switch anything.

The short version: semiconductor ETFs own the companies that make chips. Photonics ETFs own the companies that connect those chips with light. Pure optical funds such as LYTE and FOTO share very few holdings with a chip fund. The lithography fund EUV and the memory hybrid PRAM share a lot.

Two different parts of the same machine

Think of an AI data center as a city. The processors are the buildings where the work gets done. Memory chips are the warehouses. The network is the road system that connects everything. For years investors focused on the buildings. In 2026 the roads got their own funds.

Semiconductor ETFs hold the companies that design and manufacture chips, along with the companies that make the equipment for chip factories. The largest holdings are usually household names in investing circles: Nvidia, TSMC, Broadcom, AMD, ASML and the big equipment makers.

Photonics ETFs hold companies whose business is built on light: lasers, optical transceivers, fiber and the specialty materials behind them. The largest holdings are names many investors had not heard of two years ago: Lumentum, Coherent, Ciena, Fabrinet and Chinese module makers such as Zhongji Innolight. Our guide to what a photonics ETF is covers the basics.

The two industries depend on each other. Nvidia named Coherent, Corning, Fabrinet, Lumentum and TSMC among the partners building its optical switches.[8] But a stock market index has to put each company in one bucket, and the optical component makers mostly sit outside the semiconductor bucket. That classification detail is the main reason the overlap is small.

Almost every row points the same direction. Semiconductor ETFs are bigger, older, cheaper and built from larger companies. Photonics ETFs are a narrower, younger and more expensive way to own a part of the supply chain the chip funds mostly miss.

Holdings: where they overlap and where they do not

The fastest way to compare any two funds is to put their top holdings next to each other. Here are the photonics funds, starting with the ones least like a chip fund.

The pure optical funds: little overlap

$LYTE, $FOTO and $LAZR are built around optical component and laser companies. Look down their top holdings and you will find almost nothing that leads a semiconductor index.

FOTO generally requires a company to get at least half of its revenue or operating profit from photonics.[9] A rule like that screens out the big diversified chip companies by design. Nvidia and Broadcom do a lot in optics, but it is nowhere near half of what they sell.

For someone who already owns a chip fund, this group adds the most that is new. It also adds the most risk, because the companies are smaller and the portfolios are tighter.

The index fund: some overlap

$PHOX tracks an index that goes a step wider. Next to Coherent and Lumentum it holds Broadcom, Credo, Arista and Amphenol. Broadcom is a core holding of every major chip fund. The others are networking and connector companies that are not always in chip indexes either. Call it moderate overlap.

The lithography fund: heavy overlap

$EUV is the outlier. Its name comes from extreme ultraviolet lithography, the light-based process used to print the most advanced chips, and its biggest positions are the companies at the center of chip manufacturing.

TSMC, ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research and KLA made up about 41% of the fund in the issuer's November 2026 holdings.[4] Every one of them is a standard holding in the large semiconductor ETFs. The optical names, Corning, Lumentum, Coherent and Ciena, are each around 3% to 4%.

That is not a flaw. Lithography really is a photonics technology, and EUV is the cheapest fund on our list at 0.35%. But if you already own a semiconductor ETF and buy EUV for diversification, most of what you are adding is more of the same companies.

The memory hybrid: heavy overlap of a different kind

$PRAM tracks an index of 20 memory and photonics companies, roughly equal-weighted.[5] About half the index is names such as Micron, SK Hynix, Seagate, Western Digital, Broadcom and Marvell. Several of those are in chip funds already. The equal weighting is the real difference: a chip index gives Micron a small weight next to Nvidia, while PRAM gives it about the same weight as everything else.

Concentration: both are top-heavy, in different ways

People sometimes assume a sector index fund is well diversified. The big semiconductor funds are not. They are market-cap weighted, which means the largest companies get the largest weights, and Nvidia, TSMC and Broadcom together often make up a very large share of the fund. You can see the current weights on the issuers' pages.[1][2]

The difference is what sits in those top slots. A chip fund's top three are some of the most valuable companies in the world, with many product lines and customers. A pure photonics fund's top three are mid-sized companies that sell a narrow range of products to a short list of buyers. A bad quarter at one of them moves the fund more, and it happens more often.

That showed up clearly on October 22, 2026. During a broad technology selloff, the Nasdaq-100 fund $QQQ fell about 2% while the LYTE photonics ETF fell about 10%.[7] Chip funds had a rough day too, but a fund built from Lumentum, Coherent and Ciena had nowhere to hide.

Fees: photonics costs more

The largest semiconductor ETFs are cheap because they are huge index funds. $SMH and $SOXX each charge about 0.35% a year, and $SOXQ charges 0.19%.[1][2][3] Confirm the current figure on each issuer's page, since fees do get adjusted.

Photonics ETFs average 0.69%. Only one, $EUV at 0.35%, matches the chip funds.

Is the higher fee justified? There are fair arguments on both sides. Active managers picking among small global optical companies do more work than an index tracking the 25 or 30 largest chip stocks, and the funds are too small to spread their costs thin. On the other hand, a fee is the one part of your return you know in advance, and over long periods it adds up.

On a $10,000 position held ten years, the gap between a 0.35% fund and a 0.75% fund is about $400 in fees alone, before counting the growth that money would have earned.

Size, liquidity and staying power

$SMH and $SOXX each hold many billions of dollars and trade millions of shares a day. You can buy or sell them almost instantly at a price within a penny or two of fair value.

The entire photonics category holds $685M across 9 funds, and it is spread unevenly.

The larger photonics funds trade well enough for an ordinary investor. The smallest ones can have wide gaps between the buying and selling price, and a fund that stays tiny for too long may be closed by its issuer. Nobody worries about SMH closing. With the smaller photonics funds, it is a real consideration. Our risk guide covers what a closure means.

Performance: what we can and cannot say

We do not publish return comparisons between these two groups, for a simple reason: the photonics funds are less than a year old. A few months of returns tells you about the market during those months and almost nothing about the funds.

What we can say is about the stocks inside them. Through mid-September 2026, Zacks reported year-to-date gains of about 142% for Lumentum, 69% for Corning and 60% for Coherent.[6] Those are far larger moves than a broad chip index made over the same stretch. Then October took a large bite back, with several optical stocks falling 10% or more on single days.

The fair summary is that photonics stocks have recently been more volatile in both directions than the broad semiconductor group. Higher highs, lower lows. A fund built only from them inherits that.

Three ways investors combine them

There is no correct mix. These are common patterns, described for education, with the trade-off each one carries.

1. Chip fund only

Plenty of investors decide a broad semiconductor ETF is enough AI hardware exposure. It is cheap, liquid and long-established. What it misses is most of the optical layer. If photonics turns out to be where the growth is, a chip fund participates only through Broadcom, Marvell and a few others.

2. Chip fund as the core, a pure optical fund as a small add-on

This is the pairing with the least duplication. A fund such as $LYTE, $FOTO, $LAZR or $PHOX brings in companies the chip fund does not hold. The cost is a higher fee and more volatility on that slice, so people who do this usually keep it small.

3. Photonics only, using the widest fund

Some investors want one fund for the theme and nothing else in the sector. $EUV is the closest thing to a blend, since it holds the chip equipment leaders plus a smaller optical sleeve. The trade-off is that it is light on the pure optical names that give the theme its name.

One pairing that makes less sense on overlap grounds is a chip fund plus EUV, or a chip fund plus PRAM, if diversification is the goal. You would mostly be buying the same companies twice at a different weighting.

How to check overlap yourself in five minutes

  1. Pull up the top ten holdings of the fund you own. Every issuer publishes them, usually daily.
  2. Open the profile of the photonics fund you are considering. Each profile on this site shows the latest top holdings. Our holdings page shows them all together.
  3. Count the shared names and add up their weights in each fund. If the shared names are more than a third of the new fund, you are largely duplicating.
  4. Look at what is new. Ask whether the companies you would be adding are ones you actually want more of.
  5. Check the cost of the difference. If you are paying 0.75% for a fund that is half the same as your 0.35% fund, the fee on the part that is really different is higher than it looks.

The bottom line

Photonics ETFs and semiconductor ETFs are related but not interchangeable. A chip fund gives you the companies that make the processors, memory and manufacturing tools, in a cheap and liquid package with a long history. A photonics fund gives you the lasers, modules and fiber that link those chips together, in a newer, smaller, pricier and more volatile package.

If you own a chip fund and want real exposure to the optical layer, the pure optical funds are the ones that add something different. If you want the lowest fee and the largest companies, $EUV is closer to what you already have. Either way, look at the holdings before the name.

Frequently asked questions

Is a photonics ETF the same as a semiconductor ETF?

No. Semiconductor ETFs hold the companies that design and manufacture chips, led by names such as Nvidia, TSMC and Broadcom. Photonics ETFs hold the companies that make lasers, optical modules, fiber and related equipment, such as Lumentum, Coherent and Ciena. There is some overlap, but the core holdings are different.

Do SMH or SOXX hold photonics stocks?

Only at the edges. They hold chip companies with optical businesses, such as Broadcom and Marvell, but the pure optical component makers that lead photonics ETFs are generally classified outside the semiconductor industry and have little or no weight in the big chip indexes. Check current holdings on each issuer's website.

Which photonics ETF is most like a semiconductor ETF?

EUV. Its largest positions are TSMC, ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research and KLA, which are core holdings of most semiconductor funds. PRAM also overlaps heavily because about half of it is memory chip and storage companies.

Are photonics ETFs more expensive than semiconductor ETFs?

Mostly yes. The large semiconductor ETFs charge roughly 0.19% to 0.35% a year. Photonics ETFs range from 0.35% to 1.00%, with most between 0.60% and 0.75%.

Are photonics ETFs riskier than semiconductor ETFs?

Generally yes. They hold smaller companies, are more concentrated in a narrow slice of the supply chain, are much smaller funds and have less than a year of history. Semiconductor ETFs are volatile too, but they are larger, older and spread across more of the industry.

Should I own both a semiconductor ETF and a photonics ETF?

That depends on your goals and is not something we can answer for you. The useful step is to check overlap: a pure optical fund such as LYTE or FOTO adds different companies to a chip fund, while EUV mostly adds more of what a chip fund already owns.
