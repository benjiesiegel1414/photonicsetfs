Photonics ETF Risks: 9 Things to Understand Before You Buy

Photonics ETFs are new, concentrated and tied to one spending cycle. Here are the nine risks that matter most, with real numbers from the October 2026 selloff and each fund's own holdings.

By Benjie Siegel · November 20, 2026 · 13 min read · Live data

Photonics ETFs had one of the fastest starts of any new fund category. In about six months, 9 funds launched and gathered $685M. The story behind them is easy to like: AI data centers need to move data with light, and a small group of companies makes the parts.

A good story is not the same thing as a safe investment. These funds are young, concentrated and tied to a single spending cycle, and in October 2026 investors got a sharp reminder of what that means. This guide walks through the nine risks that matter most, using real numbers from the funds themselves. It is education, not advice, and it is not meant to talk you into or out of anything. The goal is that nothing here surprises you later.

If you only read one paragraph: a photonics ETF is not a diversified investment. Most hold 20 to 40 stocks, with a handful doing most of the work. Those stocks can fall 10% in a day. Some funds add extra layers of risk through swaps, private companies, leverage or options. Size any position with that in mind.

1. Volatility: what October 2026 looked like

The clearest way to understand volatility is to look at a real month. Here is what happened to optical stocks in October 2026, according to market reports from 24/7 Wall St.

On October 22, as a broader technology selloff picked up ahead of earnings, Coherent fell about 12%, Applied Optoelectronics fell about 10% and Lumentum fell about 9% in a single session. The Roundhill Photonics & Optics ETF fell about 10% that day. The Nasdaq-100 fund $QQQ fell about 2%.[1]

Read that chart again. The photonics ETF fell about five times as much as the big technology index on the same day. That is what concentration in a hot theme does.

Eight days later it happened again. On October 30, after Alphabet's results raised worries about how much the big cloud companies are spending, Applied Optoelectronics dropped about 15%, Coherent about 13%, and Lumentum and Marvell about 10% each. By that point Coherent was down about 29% in a month.[2]

It is worth keeping both halves of the picture in view. Those same stocks were still up enormously for the year. The same report listed year-to-date gains of roughly 93% for Applied Optoelectronics, 124% for Lumentum and 133% for Marvell even after the drop.[2] Stocks that can double in ten months can also give back a third in four weeks. You do not get one without the other.

The funds' own pages show the result. Defiance reported a return of about -20% for $PRAM from its late-August launch through October 31, 2026.[3] The European Defiance Photonics UCITS ETF reported about -20% since its July launch and about -18% for the month of October alone.[4] Launch timing explains a lot here. A fund that came out in May had a very different first six months than one that came out in August, close to the peak.

2. Concentration: a few stocks do most of the work

A broad U.S. stock fund holds hundreds or thousands of companies. A photonics ETF typically holds a few dozen, and the top few can dominate.

In the issuer's November 2026 holdings, Lumentum alone was about 22% of $LAZR.[5] In late October, $LYTE had roughly 60% of its exposure in just three companies: Lumentum, Coherent and Ciena.[9] When one of those companies reports a bad quarter, the fund feels it immediately.

Concentration also runs across funds. If you buy two or three photonics ETFs thinking you are spreading your risk, check the overlap first. The same names appear again and again.

Most photonics ETFs are legally classified as non-diversified, which is the label that allows these large position sizes. It is disclosed in every prospectus. It is also easy to skip past.

3. Customer concentration: everything depends on a few buyers

There is a second kind of concentration one level down. The companies inside these funds sell to a very small number of customers: the giant cloud companies building AI data centers, plus Nvidia.

Applied Optoelectronics disclosed that its ten largest customers made up 99% of its revenue in the second quarter of 2026.[2] That is an extreme case, but the pattern holds across the industry. A handful of buyers decide how much optical gear gets ordered.

This is why the October 30 drop was triggered by Alphabet's results and not by anything an optics company said. When investors worry that one of those big buyers might slow its spending, every supplier gets sold at once. The suppliers themselves were saying demand was strong. Coherent's chief executive had said in August that the company saw no signs of demand weakening, and Applied Optoelectronics said demand was outrunning its production capacity through mid-2027.[2] The stocks fell anyway. Markets trade on what might change, not on what management said last quarter.

4. Cycle risk: optical demand has always come in waves

Optical components have a long history of boom and bust. The research firm LightCounting has pointed out that the market for these parts has hit a decline about every three years on average, most recently in 2019 and 2022. Its base case in mid-2025 was growth of 30% to 35% a year through 2026, slowing to 15% to 20% a year from 2027 to 2030, which it described as a possible soft landing.[7]

Notice the word possible. Slower growth is the hopeful case in that forecast. In past cycles, customers ordered more than they needed while supply was tight, then stopped ordering while they worked through inventory. Suppliers that had built new factories for peak demand were left with too much capacity.

Nobody knows whether the AI buildout will follow that pattern, pause briefly or keep running. The point is not to predict it. The point is that every photonics ETF launched during the upswing, so none of them has a track record through a down cycle.

5. China exposure and export rules

Some of the largest makers of optical modules in the world are listed in mainland China, including Zhongji Innolight, Eoptolink and Suzhou TFC Optical. Several photonics ETFs hold them, and that choice is one of the biggest differences between funds.

China exposure cuts both ways. Those companies are major suppliers and leaving them out means missing a real part of the industry. Holding them adds risks that U.S. stocks do not carry: currency moves, different accounting and disclosure rules, and the chance that trade restrictions change what they can sell or who can own them. LightCounting noted that Chinese demand for optics paused for about two months in 2025 after new limits on Nvidia chip sales to China.[7] Policy can move this market quickly.

Export rules matter for the non-Chinese companies too. The lithography names in $EUV, such as ASML and the U.S. equipment makers, have had sales to China restricted in stages over several years.

6. Swaps and counterparty risk

Several photonics ETFs do not simply buy shares. They use total return swaps, which are contracts with a bank that pay the fund the return of a stock without the fund owning it.

  • $LYTE uses swaps for its China-listed holdings and holds Treasury bills as collateral.
  • $FOTO gets a large part of its exposure through swaps and holds cash and Treasury bills behind them.
  • $PRAM holds a single swap on its entire index plus a money market fund.
  • $EUVX holds a swap on its sister fund to get 2x daily exposure.

Swaps are a normal tool and they are legal and disclosed. They do add one more thing that can go wrong: the fund depends on the bank on the other side to pay what it owes. Funds manage that with collateral and by spreading swaps across more than one bank, and failures are rare. Still, a fund that owns shares directly has one fewer moving part than a fund that owns a contract about shares.

Swaps can also make a holdings list confusing. If you look up one of these funds and see that its biggest "holding" is a Treasury bill, that is the collateral, not the strategy. Our holdings page shows the funds by actual exposure.

7. Private companies inside public funds

ETFs are allowed to hold a limited amount of assets that do not trade on an exchange. Two photonics ETFs use that room in a meaningful way.

$LAZR reported about 9% of its assets in exposure to Anthropic, the private AI company, held through a special purpose vehicle.[5] $LUMA reported about 12% in Ayar Labs, a private optical chip developer, as its largest position.[6]

The appeal is obvious. Ordinary investors cannot buy these companies, and an ETF is a simple way in. The risks are less obvious:

  • The price is an estimate. A private company has no closing price. The fund values the position using its own process, often based on the last funding round or secondary-market trades. The value in the fund can sit unchanged for weeks and then jump.
  • It cannot be sold quickly. If many investors want their money back at once, the fund has to sell its listed stocks first. The private position then becomes a bigger share of what is left.
  • It may not be photonics. Anthropic is an AI model company. Holding it in a photonics fund is a bet on AI broadly, not on optics.
  • Extra layers can mean extra fees. Holding through a special purpose vehicle can add costs that do not show up in the headline expense ratio. The prospectus explains how each fund handles this.

None of this makes private holdings bad. It makes them different, and a 9% to 12% position is large enough to matter.

8. Small funds, wide spreads and closure risk

Assets across the 9 funds are very uneven. A few have gathered real money. Several are tiny.

Fund size affects you in three practical ways.

Trading cost. Small funds usually trade fewer shares each day, and the gap between the buying and selling price, called the bid-ask spread, tends to be wider. If you buy at the higher price and sell at the lower one, that gap is a cost on top of the expense ratio. Using limit orders instead of market orders helps.

Closure. Running an ETF costs money, and issuers close funds that do not attract enough assets. If a fund closes, you are paid the value of your shares in cash. You do not lose your investment, but you may be forced to realize a gain or loss at a time you did not pick, and you have to decide where to put the money next. With 9 funds chasing one narrow theme, it would not be unusual for some to close or merge over the next few years.

Flows can reverse. Money that rushes into a hot theme can rush out. Zacks reported $LYTE at about $254 million in assets in mid-September 2026.[8] Our data sheet currently shows $109M. Part of that drop is falling prices and part is investors leaving. Either way, a fund's size is not a fixed fact.

9. Product risk: leverage and option income

Two funds on the list are built differently from the rest, and their structure is a risk by itself.

Leveraged: 2x daily

$EUVX aims for twice the daily move of its sister fund. The key word is daily. Because the fund resets every day, its return over weeks or months is not simply double. In a choppy market it can lose money even when the fund it follows ends up flat.

What the underlying fund doesUnderlying result2x daily result
Down 10%, then up 10%-1.0%-4.0%
Ten days alternating -5% and +5%-1.2%-4.9%
Down 12% in one day-12%-24%

These are arithmetic examples, not forecasts. Given that photonics stocks really did fall 10% or more in a day twice in October, the last row is not far-fetched. Single-stock leveraged funds tied to individual optical companies exist as well, and the same math applies to them with even more force.

Option income: weekly payouts

$OPTO adds a layer of put option spreads on top of photonics stock exposure and aims to pay weekly distributions. The issuer states plainly that the strategy is not designed to reduce losses when the market falls, and that distributions may include return of capital and may be reduced or suspended.[10] In a selloff, the sold puts lose money at the same time the stocks do. A simple risk checklist for any photonics ETF

Before buying any fund on this site, you should be able to answer these questions. Every answer is on the fund's profile page here or in its prospectus.

  1. What are the top five holdings, and what share of the fund are they? If the answer is more than half, you are mostly buying those five stocks.
  2. Does it hold China-listed companies? Decide whether you want that exposure before you buy, not after a headline.
  3. Does it use swaps, leverage or options? Know which, and why.
  4. Does it hold private companies? How big, and how are they valued?
  5. How big is the fund and how does it trade? Check assets and the bid-ask spread.
  6. What does it cost? Fees here range from 0.35% to 1.00%.
  7. How much of your portfolio is already in the same stocks? If you own a semiconductor or AI fund, you may already hold some of these names. See photonics ETFs vs. semiconductor ETFs.
  8. Could you sit through a 30% drop? Individual holdings fell about that much in a month this fall. If the honest answer is no, the position is probably too big.

How investors commonly manage these risks

There is no way to remove the risk from a single-theme fund. There are sensible ways people keep it in proportion. These are general practices, not personal advice.

  • Keep it a slice, not the core. Many investors treat thematic funds as a small satellite position around a broad, diversified core.
  • Buy in pieces. Spreading purchases over several months reduces the chance of putting everything in at a peak. Investors who bought at the August launches learned this quickly.
  • Use limit orders. Especially on the smaller funds.
  • Do not stack overlapping funds. Two photonics ETFs with the same top five holdings are one bet, not two.
  • Re-check holdings. Active funds change. A fund you bought for one reason can look different a quarter later. We keep the profiles on this site updated for that reason.
  • Know your reason. If you can say in one sentence why you own it, you are less likely to sell in a panic or hold on out of stubbornness.

The bottom line

The case for photonics is real, and so are the risks. These funds hold a small group of volatile companies that all depend on the same few customers continuing to spend. Some add China exposure, swaps, private companies, leverage or options on top. October 2026 showed how fast prices can move when sentiment turns, even while the companies themselves were reporting strong demand.

None of that means photonics ETFs are a bad idea. It means they are a concentrated bet that should be sized like one. Start with the full list of funds, open the profile of any fund you are considering, and read its holdings and risk section before anything else. If a term was unfamiliar, the glossary covers it.

Frequently asked questions

Are photonics ETFs risky?

Yes. They are single-theme funds that hold a small number of volatile stocks tied to AI data center spending. In October 2026 several of the largest photonics stocks fell 10% or more in a single day, and photonics ETFs fell with them.

How much can a photonics ETF fall?

There is no floor. As one real example, the Roundhill Photonics & Optics ETF (LYTE) fell about 10% on October 22, 2026 while the Nasdaq-100 fund QQQ fell about 2%. Several funds reported losses of roughly 20% from their launch dates through October 31, 2026.

Which photonics ETF is the least risky?

No photonics ETF is low risk. Funds built from larger companies with flatter position sizes, such as the lithography-focused EUV, have generally held bigger, more established businesses than the pure optical funds, but they are still concentrated sector funds.

Do photonics ETFs hold Chinese stocks?

Some do. LYTE, PHOX and LUMA have exposure to China-listed optical module makers such as Zhongji Innolight and Eoptolink, in some cases through swaps. FOTO has been described as having no China exposure. Check each fund's holdings.

Can a photonics ETF close?

Yes. Issuers sometimes close funds that stay small. If that happens you get the value of your shares in cash, which can create a tax bill at a time you did not choose. Several photonics ETFs have less than $10 million in assets.

Why do some photonics ETFs own private companies?

A few funds use the flexibility ETFs have to hold a limited amount of illiquid assets. LAZR holds exposure to Anthropic and LUMA holds Ayar Labs. Private positions are valued by estimate and cannot be sold quickly.
