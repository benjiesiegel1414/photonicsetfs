Photonics Income ETFs: How OPTO Pays Weekly From Photonics Stocks

OPTO is the first photonics ETF built to pay weekly income. Here is how its put spread strategy works, where the payouts come from, what return of capital means and how to judge it against a plain photonics fund.

By Benjie Siegel · November 20, 2026 · 12 min read · Live data

Photonics stocks do not pay much in dividends. The companies in this industry are spending nearly everything they earn on new factories and research. So for the first few months of the photonics ETF boom, there was nothing in the category for investors who want cash flow.

That changed in October 2026, when Tuttle Capital launched the Photonics Income Blast ETF, ticker $OPTO. It is the first photonics fund built to pay distributions, and it aims to pay them every week.[1]

This guide explains how a fund can squeeze weekly income out of stocks that pay almost none, what you give up to get it, and how to judge whether the trade is working. We cover a lot of weekly and monthly income funds across our network, and the same questions apply here. As always, this is education and not a recommendation.

The short version: OPTO owns photonics exposure and sells put option spreads on the same stocks. The option premium funds the weekly payout. That income is real cash, but it is not free: the fund takes on extra downside in selloffs, payouts are not guaranteed, and some of what you receive may be your own capital coming back.

Why regular photonics ETFs pay almost nothing

An ETF can only pass along what its holdings produce. A dividend stock fund collects dividends and hands them to you. A bond fund collects interest. A photonics fund collects very little of either, because the companies it owns are in building mode.

Most of the growth-focused funds on this site list their distribution schedule as annual. $LYTE, $EUV and $LUMA all say so on their fund pages.[7] In practice an annual distribution from a fund like this is usually small, and sometimes there is none.

One partial exception is $PRAM, the memory and photonics hybrid from Defiance. It showed a 30-day SEC yield of 3.40% as of October 31, 2026 and paid a small distribution in September.[4] That fund gets its stock exposure through a swap and keeps most of its assets in a government money market fund as collateral, and money market funds pay interest. So the yield reflects how the fund is built more than anything photonics companies are paying out.

If you want meaningful, regular cash from this theme, it has to be manufactured. That is where options come in.

How OPTO is built

OPTO has two layers, and it helps to think about them separately.

The first layer is the same idea as Tuttle's original photonics fund, $FOTO. That fund invests at least 80% of its assets in companies that generally get at least half of their revenue or operating profit from photonics.[3] OPTO draws on the same list.

The second layer is what makes it an income fund, and it is different from what most people expect.

It is not a covered call fund

Most option income ETFs use covered calls. They own a stock and sell someone else the right to buy it at a higher price. They collect a premium for that, and in exchange they give up gains above that price. In a strong rally, a covered call fund gets left behind.

OPTO does not sell calls against its photonics holdings.[1] That is a deliberate choice. Photonics stocks have had enormous up moves, and capping them would defeat much of the reason to own the theme.

Instead, the income comes from puts. Here is the difference in one table.

Covered call fundPut spread fund like OPTO
What it sellsCalls on stock it ownsPut spreads on the same stocks it has exposure to
Upside on the stock layerCapped above the call strikeNot capped by the income trade
DownsideFull stock loss, cushioned a little by premiumFull stock loss plus losses on the put spreads, up to a set limit per spread
Best market for itFlat to slowly risingFlat to rising
Worst market for itSharp rally (missed gains) or sharp dropSharp drop

So OPTO trades one weakness for another. It keeps more of the upside than a covered call fund would. It takes on more of the downside.

How a put credit spread works

A put credit spread sounds complicated and is not. It is two trades made together.

  1. Sell a put near the current price. You are paid a premium. In return you promise to buy the stock at that price if it falls below it.
  2. Buy a put at a lower price. You pay a smaller premium. This is your insurance. It caps how much you can lose on the first trade.

You collect more than you pay, so you start with cash in hand. That net amount is the credit, and it is the most you can make.

Here is a made-up example with round numbers. Suppose a photonics stock trades at $100.

  • Sell the $98 put and collect $3.00
  • Buy the $90 put and pay $1.00
  • Net credit: $2.00 per share

Illustration with made-up numbers by PhotonicsETFs.com. Real option prices vary. Result shown per share at expiration.

Three outcomes are possible at expiration:

  • Stock at $98 or higher. Both puts expire worthless. You keep the full $2.00. This is the best case, and it happens even if the stock went nowhere or dipped a little.
  • Stock between $90 and $98. You give some of it back. At $96 you break even.
  • Stock at $90 or lower. You lose the most you can: the $8 gap between the strikes, minus the $2 you collected, for a $6 loss.

Look at the shape. You risk $6 to make $2. A trade like that wins most of the time and occasionally loses several times what a win pays. That lopsided shape is true of every premium-selling strategy, and it is the single most important thing to understand about income funds built on options.

The purchased put is what Tuttle means when it describes a defined maximum profit and maximum loss on each spread.[1] The loss on any single spread is capped. Losses can still add up across many spreads, week after week, in a falling market.

Where the weekly payout really comes from

Each week the fund collects premium from new spreads and pays out a distribution. If the stocks held up, that premium was earned and the payout is funded by real gains.

If the stocks fell and the spreads lost money, the fund has a choice: cut the distribution, or keep paying from its assets. When a fund pays out more than it earned, the extra is return of capital. It is your own money being handed back to you.

Tuttle says this directly. The fund expects, but does not guarantee, weekly distributions. Distributions may exceed the fund's income, may include return of capital, and may be reduced or suspended.[2]

Return of capital is not automatically bad. It can be a tax-efficient way to receive cash. But it is not a yield in the way most people use the word. If you put in $10,000 and get $1,000 of your own money back, you did not earn 10%. You have $9,000 still invested and $1,000 in your pocket.

The number that matters: total return, not yield

Every option income fund advertises a distribution rate, and the numbers can be eye-catching. The number that tells you whether the strategy is working is total return: the change in the share price plus the distributions you received.

A simple example shows why. Imagine one year in the life of three hypothetical funds that each start at $20 a share.

Fund A paid out 30% of its starting price and still left its investor with less than they started with, because the share price dropped more than the payouts added. That falling share price is often called NAV erosion or price decay. It is the thing to watch for in any high-payout fund, and it is why our sister site TopDividendETFs.com tracks price decay next to yield for every income fund it covers.

For OPTO, there is not enough history to judge yet. The fund began trading in October 2026, has only a few weeks of history, and our data sheet shows it at $554K in assets. When you check on it, compare three things over the same period: OPTO's total return, the total return of $FOTO, and OPTO's share price by itself. If OPTO's total return keeps up with FOTO's, the income is coming at little cost. If it lags badly while the share price sinks, the payouts are being funded by your own capital.

What happens to OPTO in a selloff

This is the part to be most clear-eyed about. The issuer states that the options strategy is not designed to mitigate losses in a market decline.[2]

In a drop, both layers lose at the same time. The photonics exposure falls with the stocks. The put spreads the fund sold move against it, up to the maximum loss on each one. The premium collected softens the blow a little. It does not come close to offsetting a real decline.

And real declines happen in this sector. On October 22, 2026, Coherent fell about 12%, Applied Optoelectronics about 10% and Lumentum about 9% in one session.[5] A spread sold a few percent below the market at the start of that week would have been deep in losing territory by the end of that day.

Weekly options add another wrinkle. Short-dated spreads collect premium often, which is how weekly payouts are possible. They also leave little time for a stock to recover before expiration. A sharp drop on a Wednesday can lock in the maximum loss by Friday, and then the fund has to sell new spreads at lower prices after the damage is done.

Costs and size

OPTO charges 0.99% a year.[1] That is the second highest fee among the photonics ETFs we track, and higher than FOTO's 0.75%.

Option strategies also carry trading costs that do not appear in the expense ratio. Every week the fund is buying and selling options, and each trade has a bid-ask spread. Options on smaller photonics companies are not heavily traded, so those spreads can be wide.

The fund is also very small and very new. Small funds can trade with wide spreads of their own, so limit orders are worth using. And an issuer can close a fund that does not gather assets. See our photonics ETF risk guide for more on what a closure means for shareholders.

Taxes: the basics to know

We are not tax advisors, and your situation is your own. A few general points help you ask better questions.

  • Distributions from option income funds can be a mix. Part may be ordinary income, part capital gains and part return of capital. You will not know the final split until the fund reports it after year end on Form 1099-DIV.
  • Return of capital is generally not taxed when you receive it. It reduces your cost basis instead, which means a larger taxable gain, or smaller loss, when you eventually sell. Once your basis reaches zero, further return of capital is generally taxed as a capital gain.[6]
  • Weekly payouts mean many small transactions. If you reinvest them in a taxable account, each reinvestment creates a new tax lot.
  • Account type matters. In a retirement account such as an IRA, the character of each distribution matters far less while the money stays inside the account.

Issuers publish notices that estimate the sources of each distribution. Those are worth reading, with the caveat that the estimates can change by the time final tax forms come out.

Other ways to get income near this theme

OPTO is the only dedicated photonics income ETF so far. If your goal is income first and photonics second, there are other routes investors use.

  • Pair a growth fund with a separate income fund. Some investors hold a plain photonics ETF for growth and get their cash flow from a broader income fund. That keeps the two jobs separate and lets you size each one.
  • Broad technology option income funds. Funds that sell options on the Nasdaq-100 or on semiconductor indexes pay regular income and include some of the larger optical networking names indirectly. They are far more diversified than a single-theme fund.
  • Single-stock income funds. There are option income ETFs tied to individual chip stocks. They are even more concentrated than a photonics fund and carry the same payout caveats.
  • Sell some shares on a schedule. It is not exciting, but selling a small, fixed slice of a growth fund is another way to create cash flow, and you control the amount and the timing.

You can browse weekly payers at WeeklyETFs.com and monthly payers at MonthlyETFs.com, both part of our network, to see how other option income funds have held their share prices over time.

Questions to ask before buying any option income ETF

  1. What exactly does the fund sell: calls, puts or spreads? What does that give up?
  2. Has the share price held up since launch, or has it steadily fallen?
  3. What is the total return compared with simply owning the underlying stocks or the plain version of the fund?
  4. How much of recent distributions was return of capital?
  5. What does the issuer say happens in a falling market?
  6. How large is the fund and how wide is the bid-ask spread?
  7. What is the fee, and what trading costs sit on top of it?

For OPTO specifically, the answers to questions two through four simply do not exist yet. That is not a criticism of the fund. It is what being a few weeks old means.

The bottom line

OPTO fills a real gap. It is the first way to get regular cash distributions from the photonics theme, and its design keeps more upside on the stock layer than a covered call fund would.

The cost of that income is extra exposure when photonics stocks fall, payouts that are not guaranteed, and the possibility that part of each check is your own money. Whether that trade is worth it will show up in one number over time: total return compared with the plain photonics funds.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a photonics ETF that pays dividends?

The Tuttle Capital Photonics Income Blast ETF (OPTO), launched in October 2026, is designed to pay distributions weekly using an options strategy. Most other photonics ETFs are growth funds that expect to pay little or nothing, typically once a year.

How does OPTO generate income?

OPTO keeps exposure to photonics stocks and separately sells put credit spreads on those same stocks. The premium it collects from the spreads is the main source of its distributions.

Is OPTO's distribution guaranteed?

No. The issuer says it expects to pay weekly but does not guarantee it. Distributions can be reduced or suspended and may include return of capital.

What is return of capital?

Return of capital is the part of a distribution that is your own invested money being handed back, instead of income or gains the fund earned. It is generally not taxed when received but lowers your cost basis.

Does OPTO protect against losses?

No. The issuer states that the options strategy is not designed to reduce losses in a falling market. When photonics stocks drop, both the stock exposure and the sold puts can lose money.

Is OPTO better than FOTO?

They are built for different goals. FOTO aims for growth from photonics stocks. OPTO uses the same stock universe and adds an income strategy that pays out cash and can change the total return. Neither is better by default.
