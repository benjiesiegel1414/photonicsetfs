Open the holdings of any photonics ETF and you will see the same names again and again. There are only a few dozen public companies in the world that make the lasers, optical modules and specialty materials AI data centers depend on, so the {{n}} funds we track are largely fishing in the same small pond.

That makes it worth knowing who these companies are. This guide goes through the main photonics stocks one layer of the supply chain at a time: what each company makes, why funds own it, and which ETFs hold the most of it. It describes what the funds hold. It is not a list of stock picks, and nothing here is a recommendation.

How to use this guide: skim the supply chain map first, then jump to the companies you keep seeing in fund holdings. Every fund's current top positions are on our photonics ETF holdings page, and each stock there links back to the funds that own it.

A few things jump out. Lumentum and Coherent are close to universal. The next tier is a mix of U.S. optical networking companies, European and Taiwanese materials suppliers, and Chinese module makers. And the weights vary wildly: the same stock can be 3% of one fund and more than 20% of another.

That last point matters more than the count. Owning a fund where one company is a fifth of the portfolio is a very different thing from owning a fund where it is one of thirty roughly equal positions.

A map of the photonics supply chain

It helps to sort these companies by what they do. Tuttle Capital, which launched the first pure photonics ETF, describes the industry in five layers: lasers, transceivers, silicon photonics, specialty wafers, and foundries and equipment.{{s:5}} We add two more that the funds also hold: networks and fiber, and the chip companies at the edge of the theme.

Lasers and components: the core of every fund

Lumentum ($LITE)

If photonics ETFs have a center of gravity, it is Lumentum. The California company makes the laser chips that go inside high-speed transceivers, along with complete modules and optical switching products. It was spun out of JDS Uniphase in 2015.

Its recent numbers explain the attention. For the quarter ended March 28, 2026, Lumentum reported revenue of $808.4 million, up about 90% from a year earlier, and guided the following quarter to between $960 million and $1.01 billion. It disclosed a $2 billion direct investment from Nvidia, a multi-year optical switch agreement worth billions, and the purchase of a North Carolina factory to become its fifth indium phosphide fab. Management said the company was shipping well below what customers wanted.{{s:1}}

Lumentum is the largest position in {{link:LYTE}} and {{link:LAZR}}, at more than 20% of each in the latest holdings we collected, and the largest in {{link:FOTO}} counting swap exposure.{{s:7}} It is also in {{link:PHOX}}, {{link:LUMA}} and {{link:EUV}}. If you own almost any photonics fund, you own Lumentum.

{{holdings:LYTE}}

Coherent ($COHR)

Coherent is the other name on nearly every list. It is broader than Lumentum: it sells data center transceivers, lasers for industrial and scientific use, and engineered materials. The current company was formed when II-VI bought the original Coherent in 2022 and took its name.

That breadth cuts both ways. The data center business is growing fast, while the industrial laser business follows the slower manufacturing economy. Coherent's chief executive said in August 2026 that the company saw no signs of customer demand weakening. The stock still fell about 29% in the month through October 30, 2026 as investors worried about cloud spending.{{s:3}} It is a top-three holding in LYTE and PHOX and appears in every other pure photonics fund.

Applied Optoelectronics ($AAOI)

This Texas company makes its own laser chips and builds them into transceivers for data centers and cable broadband. It is much smaller than the two above, which makes it more volatile. It also shows the industry's customer concentration at its most extreme: its ten largest customers were 99% of revenue in the second quarter of 2026.{{s:3}} It appears in LYTE and has been among FOTO's holdings.

MACOM ($MTSI) and Semtech ($SMTC)

These two make the analog chips that drive lasers and clean up signals inside optical modules and cables. They are semiconductor companies by classification, but a large and growing part of their sales goes into optical links. MACOM is a top holding in {{link:LUMA}} and Semtech in {{link:LAZR}}.

Modules: the Chinese transceiver makers

Zhongji Innolight and Eoptolink

Two of the largest makers of data center transceivers in the world trade on the Shenzhen stock exchange. Zhongji Innolight and Eoptolink take laser chips and other parts and assemble them into the finished modules that plug into switches. Nvidia has named both among its transceiver partners.{{s:4}}

Whether a fund holds these companies is one of the clearest dividing lines in the category. {{link:LYTE}} and {{link:PHOX}} hold both, plus a third Chinese supplier, Suzhou TFC Optical. {{link:LUMA}} holds Eoptolink. {{link:FOTO}} and {{link:EUV}} have neither in their top holdings.

{{holdings:PHOX}}

Owning them gives a fund a fuller picture of the industry. It also brings currency risk, different disclosure rules and exposure to trade policy between the U.S. and China. Some funds reach these stocks through swaps instead of buying the shares directly. Our risk guide explains what that means.

Manufacturing and foundries

Fabrinet ($FN)

Fabrinet does not design optical products. It builds them for other companies, mainly in Thailand, and it is known for the kind of precision assembly optical parts require. That makes it a way to own the growth of the whole industry without betting on one product. It is on Nvidia's partner list for both co-packaged optics and transceivers,{{s:4}} and it shows up in LYTE and FOTO.

Tower Semiconductor ($TSEM) and Win Semiconductors

Optical chips need specialized factories. Tower, based in Israel, runs a silicon photonics manufacturing platform that many optical chip designers use. Win Semiconductors in Taiwan is a foundry for compound semiconductors. Both appear in LAZR, and Tower is in LUMA as well.

TSMC ($TSM)

The world's largest chip foundry is not a photonics company in the usual sense, but it matters twice over. It is the biggest customer for the lithography machines that give {{link:EUV}} its name, and it is the manufacturing partner for Nvidia's co-packaged optics.{{s:4}} It is EUV's largest holding at about 13%.{{s:8}}

Materials and tools: the picks and shovels

Lasers for optical communications are not made from silicon. They are made from compound materials, mainly indium phosphide, grown in thin layers on special wafers. Only a handful of companies supply those wafers and the machines that process them.

Aixtron

This German company makes the deposition equipment used to grow the crystal layers inside lasers. Every company adding laser capacity needs these machines. Aixtron is the second largest holding in both LAZR and LUMA and is in PHOX's top ten.{{s:7}}{{s:9}}{{s:10}}

AXT Inc ($AXTI)

AXT makes indium phosphide wafers, the starting material for laser chips. Supply has been tight enough that lead times for some laser parts stretched past 2027, according to research cited by Tuttle.{{s:5}} AXT's manufacturing is in China, which adds export-permit and trade risk on top of normal business risk. It is a top-five holding in LAZR.

LandMark Optoelectronics and Soitec

LandMark, in Taiwan, supplies the pre-grown wafers laser makers start from. Soitec, in France, makes the engineered silicon wafers used for silicon photonics. Both are in LUMA, and LandMark is in LAZR.

{{holdings:LAZR}}

Funds that lean on this layer, LAZR above all, are making a slightly different bet than funds built on module makers. Materials suppliers sell to everyone, so they do well when total laser volume grows, whichever design wins. They are also small, thinly traded and listed on foreign exchanges.

Networks and fiber

Ciena ($CIEN)

Ciena, based in Maryland, makes the optical systems that carry data between data centers and across continents. For most of its history it sold to phone companies. Now the large cloud companies are major customers as they link AI sites together. It is a top-three position in LYTE and FOTO and a smaller one in EUV.

Corning ($GLW)

Corning invented low-loss optical fiber in 1970 and is still one of the largest makers of fiber and cable. AI data centers use far more fiber than older designs. Nvidia committed $500 million to Corning as part of at least $6.5 billion it put into photonics suppliers over a three-month stretch in 2026, according to CNBC reporting cited by Zacks, alongside $2 billion each to Lumentum, Coherent and Marvell.{{s:2}}

Corning is a diversified glass company, so optical fiber is only part of its business. It appears in EUV and in the index behind Europe's photonics UCITS fund, where it was the second largest weight.{{s:6}}

Nokia ($NOK)

Nokia became one of the largest optical networking vendors after buying Infinera. It is a top-ten position in LUMA.

Chips at the edge of the theme

Several large semiconductor companies have real optical businesses without being photonics companies.

  • Broadcom ($AVGO) designs the switch chips at the center of data center networks and sells optical components and co-packaged optics. It is in PHOX and PRAM.
  • Marvell ($MRVL) makes the signal processing chips inside most high-speed transceivers. It is in PRAM and in the European photonics index.
  • Credo ($CRDO) makes high-speed connectivity chips and active electrical cables. It is in PHOX and PRAM.
  • Arista ($ANET) and Amphenol ($APH) make network switches and connectors. Both are in PHOX.

Index funds tend to include this group, and strict pure-play funds tend to leave it out. The index behind the European photonics fund, for example, defines a pure play as a company with at least 50% of revenue from photonics and fills its list with those first.{{s:6}} How strictly a fund draws that line shapes what you end up owning. Lithography and industrial lasers

ASML ($ASML) and the chip equipment makers

Lithography is photonics too. ASML's machines use extreme ultraviolet light to print the smallest features on advanced chips, and it is the only company that makes them. {{link:EUV}} is built around ASML, TSMC and the big U.S. equipment makers Applied Materials, Lam Research and KLA.

{{holdings:EUV}}

These are large, established businesses, and they behave more like the semiconductor sector than like the optical networking stocks. See photonics ETFs vs. semiconductor ETFs for what that means if you already own a chip fund.

IPG Photonics ($IPGP) and nLIGHT ($LASR)

Not every laser goes into a data center. IPG makes high-power fiber lasers for cutting and welding metal. nLIGHT makes lasers for industry and for defense, including directed energy systems. {{link:FOTO}} is the fund that leans on this group, with IPG its second largest exposure in the latest holdings we collected. Industrial lasers follow factory spending more than AI spending, so they add a different driver to the fund.

{{holdings:FOTO}}

The private companies

Two funds hold companies you cannot buy on an exchange. {{link:LUMA}}'s largest position is Ayar Labs, a startup developing optical links between chips.{{s:9}} {{link:LAZR}} holds exposure to Anthropic, the AI model developer, through a special purpose vehicle.{{s:7}} Ayar Labs is squarely a photonics company. Anthropic is not; it is a customer of the whole AI hardware chain. Both positions are valued by estimate instead of by a daily market price.

What these stocks have in common

Step back from the individual names and four shared traits stand out. Each is worth knowing whether you own these companies directly or through a fund.

  1. The same few customers. Demand comes from a short list of cloud companies and from Nvidia. When investors worry about one of them, all of these stocks fall together.
  2. Capacity is the limit. Through 2026, the story across the industry was that suppliers could not make enough. Lumentum's chief executive said in October that optical components were sold out through early 2029.{{s:3}} Tight supply supports prices. It also tempts everyone to build factories at once, which is how past optical cycles ended.
  3. Big moves in both directions. By late October 2026, Lumentum was still up about 124% for the year and Marvell about 133%, even after falling roughly 10% in one day.{{s:3}}
  4. Global supply chains. These companies are spread across the U.S., China, Taiwan, Thailand, Germany, France and Israel. Trade rules and currencies matter.

Stocks or an ETF?

This is a personal decision and we will not make it for you. Here is how the two approaches differ in practice.

What you ownExactly the companies you chooseA basket of roughly 20 to 40, chosen by a manager or index
Single-company riskHigh. One earnings report can move your position 15%Lower, though top holdings can still be 20% of a fund
Foreign and private namesHard to buy for most U.S. investorsIncluded in several funds

One middle path some investors take is to use an ETF for the hard-to-reach parts of the chain, such as the Asian and European suppliers, and decide separately whether they want more of the large U.S. names. Whatever you do, check for doubling up. If you own Lumentum shares and a fund that is 22% Lumentum, your real exposure is bigger than either position looks.

The bottom line

Photonics ETFs are built from a small cast of companies. Lumentum and Coherent are in nearly all of them. Around that core, each fund makes its own choices: Chinese module makers or not, materials suppliers or not, big chip companies or not, private companies or not.

Knowing the cast makes those choices easier to read. When you open a fund's holdings and recognize what each name does, the differences between funds stop being abstract. Start with every photonics ETF compared, then open the holdings for the ones that interest you.
